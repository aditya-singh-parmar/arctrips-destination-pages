Whistler

Whistler is a mountain resort town in British Columbia's Coast Mountains, known for Whistler Blackcomb, a walkable village, and hiking, biking and lake days in summer.

Why go

Why go to Whistler

Whistler is one of Canada's most famous mountain destinations, set in the Coast Mountains of British Columbia. Originally a logging community, it grew into a popular destination after being chosen as a host site for the 2010 Winter Olympics. The village is walkable, with lifts, restaurants, shops and patios close together, so you can enjoy the mountains without a car.

Whistler is more than a winter resort. In summer it becomes a playground for hiking, biking, lake days, alpine sightseeing, ziplining, golf and paddling. In fall it is quieter and scenic, and in winter the village comes alive with snow, lights, après-ski and mountain energy.

Where it is
In the Coast Mountains of British Columbia, with the village right at the base of Whistler and Blackcomb mountains.
Getting there
About two to two and a half hours from Vancouver on the Sea to Sky Highway. Winter driving needs more planning.
Known for
Whistler Blackcomb, skiing and snowboarding, the Peak 2 Peak Gondola, mountain biking, alpine hiking, lakes and après-ski.
When to come
December to February for snow, July and August for biking and hiking. Spring and fall are quieter.
When to go

When to go to Whistler

Whistler is busiest from December to February, around holidays and long weekends, and again from late June through August. April, May, October and November are quieter, and every season has a reason to come.

Summer, June to August

Alpine sightseeing, hiking, lakes and biking. Late June through August is one of the busiest seasons, so book rooms, rentals and tours early. Dress in layers: the alpine is cooler than the village.

Fall, September to November

September is excellent for fewer crowds. October and November are quieter and cheaper, though some attractions run reduced hours, and the ski season begins in late November.

Winter, December to February

The classic Whistler season: skiing, snowboarding, après-ski and a festive village. It is busiest and most expensive around Christmas, New Year, long weekends and school holidays. January can feel quieter.

Spring, March to May

March can bring longer days and spring skiing. In April the village begins to shift toward spring, and April and May are quieter, with some attractions on reduced hours.

No month is a bad month here if you dress for it: layers and a waterproof shell cover most of the year.

Things to do in Whistler

The first-time list: gondola views, the village, culture and a spa day, in any season.

Ride the Peak 2 Peak Gondola

The Peak 2 Peak Gondola connects Whistler Mountain and Blackcomb Mountain and gives huge views of forests, valleys, peaks and Whistler Village below. You do not need to ski or hike to ride it, so it suits first-time visitors, families and photographers. It is a top attraction in both winter and summer.

Good to know. Hours and access change by season, so check the current schedule. Mountain temperatures are often 5 to 10°C cooler than the village.

Explore Whistler Village

Whistler Village is the heart of the resort. It is walkable and easy to enjoy without a car, with shops, restaurants, cafes, bars, galleries and lift bases side by side. In winter it feels cozy and festive, and in summer patios, bikes and outdoor events fill the evenings.

Good to know. Stay near the village for the easiest access to restaurants, shops, lifts and tours.

Visit the Squamish Lil'wat Cultural Centre

The Squamish Lil'wat Cultural Centre is one of the most meaningful places to visit in Whistler. It shares the living cultures, stories, art and traditions of the Squamish Nation and Lil'wat Nation. It is a good rainy-day stop and a strong choice for families, and it helps visitors understand the land beyond the resort experience.

Good to know. Give yourself enough time to explore the exhibits and consider joining a guided experience if available.

Relax at Scandinave Spa

Scandinave Spa is one of Whistler's most popular wellness experiences, known for outdoor hot baths, cold plunges, steam rooms, saunas, quiet spaces and a peaceful forest setting. It is a great choice after skiing, hiking, biking or a long travel day, in summer and in winter.

Good to know. The spa is quiet and phone-free, so it suits adults looking for calm. Book ahead because it is popular in both seasons.

Visit the Audain Art Museum

The Audain Art Museum is a strong cultural stop close to Whistler Village. It features art from British Columbia, including important Indigenous art and works by major regional artists. It is a good choice when the weather is rainy, or when you want a slower day or a break from outdoor activities.

Good to know. Pair it with the Squamish Lil'wat Cultural Centre for a culture-focused day.

Paddle on Whistler's Lakes

Lost Lake, Alta Lake, Green Lake and Nita Lake are all popular in summer. Alta Lake is especially good for paddleboarding, canoeing and kayaking, with Wayside Park, Lakeside Park and Rainbow Park as access points. Nita Lake is quieter and scenic.

Good to know. Lake water can still be cold early in the season. Wind can pick up in the afternoon, so beginners should choose calm conditions.

Questions about things to do in Whistler

What is the best thing to do in Whistler for first-time visitors?

The Peak 2 Peak Gondola is one of the best first-time activities because it gives you big mountain views and a true Whistler experience without needing special skills.

What can you do in Whistler on a rainy day?

Visit the Audain Art Museum, the Squamish Lil'wat Cultural Centre, Scandinave Spa, cafes, shops and restaurants, or take a shorter forest walk if conditions are safe.

Is the Peak 2 Peak Gondola open in summer?

Yes, it usually operates as part of Whistler Blackcomb's summer sightseeing experience, but dates and hours change by season. Check current lift operations before going.

Is Whistler good for families?

Yes. Families can enjoy the village, gondola sightseeing, Lost Lake, easy biking, snowshoeing, ski lessons, lakes, museums and guided activities.

Skiing and snowboarding in Whistler

Whistler Blackcomb is one resort on two mountains, joined by the Peak 2 Peak Gondola, with terrain from learning zones to glaciers.

Whistler Mountain

Whistler Mountain is often the classic starting point for visitors staying in Whistler Village or Creekside. It has a strong mix of groomed runs, alpine terrain, tree skiing, learning areas and scenic routes. Beginners can use learning zones, intermediates can enjoy long groomers, and advanced riders can explore steeper alpine terrain.

Good to know. Whistler Mountain is large. Beginners should stay close to marked beginner areas and avoid following faster riders into advanced terrain.

Blackcomb Mountain

Blackcomb Mountain sits beside Whistler Mountain and offers another huge section of terrain. It is known for varied skiing, wide groomers, alpine zones and glacier scenery, with strong options for intermediate and advanced riders. It also has beginner and intermediate runs.

Good to know. Blackcomb can be a great second-day mountain after you have warmed up on Whistler.

Beginner Skiing and Lessons

Whistler can be beginner-friendly, but it is important to start in the right place. Beginners should take lessons, use learning areas and stick to green runs until they are comfortable stopping, turning, controlling speed and getting on and off lifts. Whistler Blackcomb offers group and private lessons, including first-timer programs.

Good to know. Book lessons ahead during busy winter periods. Wear waterproof clothing, warm gloves, goggles and a helmet.

Terrain Parks

Whistler Blackcomb has terrain park options for freestyle skiers and snowboarders, with features such as jumps, rails, boxes and progression lines. Terrain parks are not only for experts, but riders should choose features that match their level. Start small, watch how others approach a feature, and never stop in landing zones.

Good to know. Follow park rules, start with small features and use proper speed control.

Tree Skiing and Powder Days

Tree skiing can be one of the most fun parts of Whistler, especially on storm days when alpine visibility is poor. Trees provide contrast, shelter and powder pockets. It is not beginner terrain: it takes strong control, quick turning and awareness of tree wells, branches and hidden obstacles.

Good to know. Never ski trees alone. Tree wells can be dangerous, especially after heavy snowfall.

Après-Ski and Dining

After a day on the mountain, people gather in the village for drinks, food, music, patios with heaters, casual pubs and cozy restaurants. You can enjoy après-ski even if you did not ski, with everything from coffee and bakeries to fine dining, cocktail bars and family restaurants.

Good to know. Popular restaurants and après spots get busy. Make reservations for dinner, especially on weekends and holidays.

Questions about skiing and snowboarding in Whistler

Is Whistler Blackcomb one ski resort or two?

One resort made up of two mountains, Whistler Mountain and Blackcomb Mountain. They are connected by the Peak 2 Peak Gondola.

Which is better, Whistler Mountain or Blackcomb Mountain?

Both are excellent. Whistler Mountain is often a classic choice for first-time visitors and village access. Blackcomb is also large and varied, with strong intermediate and advanced terrain. Many visitors try both.

When is the best time to ski in Whistler?

The best time for a ski-focused trip is usually January to March. December is festive but busy, and April can offer spring skiing with more variable conditions.

Do you need to book lift tickets in advance?

Booking lift tickets in advance is recommended, especially during holidays, weekends and peak ski dates. It can also help you avoid last-minute stress.

Is Whistler good for beginners?

Yes, especially with lessons. The resort is large and can feel overwhelming, so book lessons, rent proper gear, stay near learning areas and avoid following more experienced friends into unfamiliar terrain.

Hiking in Whistler

Easy lake loops and forest walks near the village, gondola-accessed alpine trails, and big day hikes into Garibaldi Provincial Park.

Lost Lake Loop

The main Lost Lake loop is about 4 km, with minimal elevation gain, and usually takes 1 to 1.5 hours at a relaxed pace. You can add nearby forest trails for a longer walk. It is beautiful in summer, when people swim, picnic and bike nearby; in winter it becomes a Nordic skiing and snowshoeing area.

Good to know. Lost Lake can be busy in summer. Go early or later in the day for a quieter walk.

Train Wreck Trail

Train Wreck is one of the most unique hikes in Whistler. The trail leads through forest to old painted train boxcars scattered near the Cheakamus River, and crosses a suspension-style bridge. It is about 5 km round trip, with minimal elevation gain, and usually takes 1 to 3 hours. There are roots and some uneven sections.

Good to know. Use the official trail access. Do not walk on railway tracks.

Cheakamus Lake Trail

Cheakamus Lake is one of the best moderate lake hikes near Whistler. The trail follows forest and river scenery to a turquoise lake surrounded by mountains. It is about 14 km round trip, with around 400 m of elevation gain, and usually takes 4 to 5 hours. The climb is more gradual than many alpine hikes.

Good to know. Cheakamus Lake is in Garibaldi Provincial Park, so check current access, parking and pass requirements before going.

High Note Trail

High Note Trail is one of the best alpine hikes in Whistler. It starts from the top of Whistler Mountain and gives huge views over Cheakamus Lake, Black Tusk, Garibaldi Provincial Park and alpine meadows. The full trail is about 9 to 10 km, with about 430 m of elevation change, and takes 3 to 4.5 hours. It is moderate to hard.

Good to know. You need lift access, and the trail is only available when mountain operations and conditions allow. Bring layers because the alpine is cooler than the village.

Garibaldi Lake

Garibaldi Lake is one of the most famous hikes in the Sea to Sky area, and many Whistler visitors include it as a big hiking day. It is about 18 km round trip, with around 820 m of elevation gain, and takes 5 to 7+ hours. The trail climbs steadily through forest to a bright blue glacier-fed lake.

Good to know. Garibaldi Provincial Park may require a free day-use pass during busy periods. Check BC Parks rules before going.

Wedgemount Lake

Wedgemount Lake is one of the hardest popular hikes near Whistler. It is steep, rocky and demanding, but the reward is a bright alpine lake below glacier-covered peaks. The hike is about 12 to 14 km round trip, with around 1,200 m of elevation gain, and takes 6 to 8+ hours.

Good to know. This is much harder than the distance suggests. Do not choose it as your first Whistler hike.

Questions about hiking in Whistler

What is the best hike in Whistler?

For easy hiking, Lost Lake and Train Wreck are two of the best choices. For alpine views, High Note Trail is one of the best. For a hard full-day lake hike, Garibaldi Lake, Rainbow Lake and Wedgemount Lake are popular.

What is the easiest hike in Whistler?

Lost Lake Loop, the Valley Trail, Train Wreck, Rainbow Falls and Brandywine Falls are some of the easiest hikes and walks in Whistler.

Do you need a pass to hike in Whistler?

Some nearby hikes in Garibaldi Provincial Park may require free day-use passes during busy periods. Lift-access alpine hikes require sightseeing or lift access. Always check current rules before going.

Is Wedgemount Lake hard?

Yes. Wedgemount Lake is very hard, with a steep climb, rough terrain and major elevation gain. It is best for strong, prepared hikers.

When is the best time to hike in Whistler?

The best hiking season is usually June to October. Lower trails open earlier, while alpine trails are best once the snow has melted and lifts are operating.

Biking in Whistler

Lift-access downhill at Whistler Mountain Bike Park, plus easy paved and forest riding for everyone else.

Fitzsimmons Zone

The Fitzsimmons Zone is the original and most famous part of the bike park. It is close to Whistler Village and is where many riders start. It has beginner-friendly progression trails, classic flow trails, technical trails and some of the most famous lines in the park.

Good to know. Do not judge bike park trails by ski-run colors alone. A green downhill bike trail can still feel harder than a normal paved or gravel ride.

Garbanzo Zone

Garbanzo takes riders higher up the mountain. Trails here are longer, rougher and more demanding than the lower mountain. Riders should be comfortable with sustained downhill riding, braking, body position and fatigue. It suits intermediate to advanced riders who want longer descents.

Good to know. Take a lesson and rent proper gear if you are new to the bike park.

Peak Zone and Top of the World

The Peak Zone gives riders a high-alpine experience with big mountain views. Top of the World is one of Whistler's most iconic alpine bike trails, but it is not for beginners. The terrain is exposed, rocky and more serious than lower park trails.

Good to know. Best for advanced and expert riders after time on the lower park.

The Valley Trail

The Valley Trail is one of the best ways to bike in Whistler if you are not looking for downhill riding. It is a paved, car-free trail network that connects Whistler Village with lakes, parks, neighborhoods, viewpoints and picnic areas. It is the best choice for families, casual riders and couples.

Good to know. The trail is shared by walkers, runners, cyclists, children and dogs. Ride slowly in busy areas and use your bell when passing.

Lost Lake Biking

Lost Lake is one of the easiest places to enjoy biking close to Whistler Village. The area has paved access through the Valley Trail, plus forest trails nearby. Casual riders can bike to the lake for swimming, a picnic or a short loop, and more confident riders can explore nearby cross-country trails.

Good to know. Book rentals and lessons early during summer weekends, holidays and bike events.

Crankworx Whistler

Crankworx Whistler is the biggest mountain biking event in Whistler and one of the most important bike festivals in the world. It is usually held in summer, most often in July or August, and brings professional riders, races, demos, Kidsworx events and a high-energy village atmosphere.

Good to know. Whistler gets very busy during Crankworx. Book lodging, rentals and restaurants early, and check which bike park zones are affected.

Questions about biking in Whistler

Is Whistler Bike Park good for beginners?

Yes, but approach it carefully. It has beginner trails, lessons and progression routes, but it is still a downhill park with steeper terrain and higher speeds. Rent proper gear, take a lesson and start on green trails.

Do you need a special bike for Whistler Bike Park?

Yes. Use a downhill or enduro mountain bike with proper suspension and brakes. A casual bike, cruiser or basic hybrid is not suitable for downhill park trails.

What is the easiest biking in Whistler?

The Valley Trail. It is paved, scenic and connects the village with lakes, parks and neighborhoods.

When is the best time to bike in Whistler?

The best biking season is usually summer, especially June through September. Fall can also be excellent, depending on weather, trail conditions and lift operations.

Can you e-bike in Whistler?

Yes. E-bikes are available and popular for scenic rides, the Valley Trail and guided tours. Check local rules because not all trails allow all types of e-bikes.

Winter beyond skiing in Whistler

Snow days for non-skiers and rest days: snowshoeing, tubing, Nordic skiing, snowmobile tours and skating.

Go Snowshoeing

Snowshoeing is one of the best winter activities in Whistler for non-skiers. It lets you walk through snowy forest trails without needing downhill ski skills. You can snowshoe on maintained trails or book a guided tour. It is slower than skiing, which is part of the appeal.

Good to know. Wear warm waterproof boots, layers, gloves and a hat. Trails may require passes or rentals depending on where you go.

Try Tubing

Tubing is one of the easiest snow activities in Whistler. You do not need ski or snowboard experience: you sit in an inflatable tube and slide down a groomed snow lane. It is a fun option for families, groups and visitors who want a simple winter activity without lessons.

Good to know. Dress warmly and wear waterproof clothing. Check height, age and operating rules before going.

Cross-Country Ski at Lost Lake

Lost Lake is popular in summer, but in winter it becomes a Nordic skiing and snowshoeing area. It is close to the village and offers a quieter snow experience than the downhill slopes. Cross-country skiing is a good choice if you want exercise, winter scenery and a slower pace.

Good to know. Trail access, rentals and passes may be required. Check current Lost Lake winter operations before going.

Try Snowmobiling

Snowmobiling is one of Whistler's classic winter adventure tours. Guided trips take you through snowy forest roads, mountain terrain and scenic winter areas. It is a good activity if you want something faster and more adventurous than snowshoeing but do not want to ski or snowboard.

Good to know. Check age, driver's license, clothing and safety requirements before booking. Tours can sell out during peak winter dates.

Go Ice Skating

Ice skating is a fun, classic winter activity for families, couples and non-skiers. Whistler usually offers seasonal outdoor skating in the village area, depending on weather and operations. It is a good option if you want something easy and festive without leaving town.

Good to know. Check the current rink location, hours and rental information before going.

Questions about winter beyond skiing in Whistler

What can you do in Whistler in winter besides skiing?

You can ride the Peak 2 Peak Gondola, go snowshoeing, try tubing, cross-country ski, relax at Scandinave Spa, visit museums, go snowmobiling, go ziplining, ice skate, walk the village and enjoy après-ski.

Is Whistler worth visiting if you do not ski?

Yes. The village, mountain views, snowshoeing, tubing, gondola sightseeing, spa, museums, winter tours, dining and après-ski make it a strong winter destination for non-skiers.

Can non-skiers ride the Peak 2 Peak Gondola?

Yes, for winter sightseeing when sightseeing access is operating. Check current lift and ticket details before going.

Is Whistler good for families in winter?

Yes. Families can ski, snowboard, take lessons, go tubing, ice skate, snowshoe, enjoy hot chocolate stops, walk the village and visit indoor attractions.

Before you go

Can you visit Whistler as a day trip from Vancouver?

Yes, but it is a long day. The drive from Vancouver to Whistler usually takes about two to two and a half hours. Start early and keep your plan simple.

How many days do you need in Whistler?

One day is enough for a quick village and gondola visit. Two to three days is better if you want outdoor activities, lake time, skiing, biking or a spa visit.

Do you need a car in Whistler?

Not always. If you stay near Whistler Village, you can walk to many restaurants, shops, lifts, bike rentals and tours. The Valley Trail also makes it easy to reach lakes and parks by bike or on foot.

Is it hard to drive to Whistler in winter?

The Sea to Sky Highway is scenic but can have rain, snow, ice and heavy traffic in winter. Check road reports and make sure your vehicle meets winter tire or chain requirements.

Is Whistler expensive?

It can be, especially for hotels, lift tickets, restaurants and tours, and prices are highest during holidays, weekends and peak ski dates. Visit midweek, avoid Christmas and New Year, and book early to save.
