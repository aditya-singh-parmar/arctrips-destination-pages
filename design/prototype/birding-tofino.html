Things to do in Tofino

Birding in Tofino

Two shorebird passages cross this coast every year, and for a few weeks in spring the tide line fills. The month you come decides almost everything you see, which is what this page is for.

When to come

Two passages, and a coast that never empties

Shorebirds move through in spring and again in fall, and those weeks are the reason to time a trip rather than take whatever week is free. No month is a write-off. Summer is when the offshore seabirds are reachable by boat, and winter is when the sea ducks, loons and grebes arrive.

Spring

The best of the year. Migrating shorebirds pass through and everything else turns active. Sandpipers, plovers, whimbrels, black turnstones, gulls, bald eagles, surf scoters and cormorants. Late April and May are the weeks to aim at.

Summer

The boat season. Calm enough to get out to the offshore rocks and islands, which is where tufted puffins, common murres, rhinoceros auklets and pigeon guillemots are. Gulls and eagles carry on along the shore.

Fall

The second passage, and fewer people watching it. Shorebirds move south while loons, grebes, scoters and harlequin ducks arrive for the winter. Good bird activity without the summer crowd.

Winter

Wet and windy, and still excellent. Surf scoters, harlequin ducks, common and Pacific loons, horned and western grebes, cormorants, gulls and bald eagles. Bring warm layers and waterproofs.

Where to stand

Six places, ordered by how far you have to go

The first is a five minute walk out of town. The last needs a boat. Everything in between is reachable by car with a short walk at the end, and all six are better within an hour or two of low tide.

All nine subjects in Tofino

Tofino Harbour

Gulls on the docks, cormorants drying their wings on the pilings, eagles watching the boats from the treeline. The one place you can bird with a coffee in your hand.

Chesterman Beach

Sand, rock and forest edge in one walk, so gulls, shorebirds at the waterline and eagles in the trees behind are all on the same short loop.

Long Beach

The open sand between the two towns, and the place the spring flocks come down on in their thousands. At low tide the feeding line runs for kilometres.

Wickaninnish Beach

The park end of the same sand, with a visitor centre, parking and a sheltered place to stand when the wind is up. Good for shorebirds and for scanning the water beyond the surf.

Grice Bay and the mudflats

The sheltered inlet side, where the tide uncovers the flats the shorebirds are actually coming for. Calm water, no surf, and the richest feeding ground on this coast.

Puffins, murres, auklets and shearwaters spend their time past the surf line and are hard to pick out from the sand. The wildlife boats go to the rocks and islands they sit on.

What you will see

Eighteen birds, ordered from the ones you cannot miss to the ones that need a boat

The first three are on every beach in Tofino on any day of the year, and you will see them without trying. The last six are past the surf line and are the reason people book a boat. Every entry says where it is, the one clue that separates it from the bird beside it, and the month to come for it.

On any beach, any month

01 to 03

No plan needed and no binoculars needed. These three are the ones a visitor notices from the car park.

Beaches, docks, rocksGrey and white, loud, everywhere

Gulls

The easiest birds to see here, on beaches, docks, rocks, rooftops and over the water. Most visitors stop at the word seagull, but there are several species mixed in the same flock: glaucous-winged, western, California, short-billed, and Heermann's in season.

Think of them as the beach cleanup crew. Grey and white bodies, long wings, strong bills, loud calls, always in groups near food.

When: every month of the year, with no tour needed.

Good to know. Glaucous-winged gulls, the common one here, show soft grey wingtips rather than the sharp black ones. Do not feed them: it makes them aggressive and unhealthy.

Tall trees, harbour, shorelineHuge, white head on adults

Bald eagle

Not a seabird, but the coastal bird most visitors most want to see, and one of the reliable ones. Look in the tallest trees along the shore, over the harbour, and on the beach itself.

Think of the eagle as the top watcher of the coast: it sits high and watches everything below. White head and tail, dark body, huge wings, slow and powerful flight.

When: all twelve months, from shore.

Good to know. Young bald eagles have no white head yet and stay mostly brown for several years, so a big brown raptor over the beach is very often the same bird.

Rocks, pilings, open waterDark body, long neck, dives

Cormorants

Dark seabirds sitting on rocks, docks and pilings, or flying low and straight over the water. They are strong swimmers and dive to catch fish. Pelagic, double-crested and Brandt's cormorants all turn up around Tofino.

Think of them as underwater hunters that look like dark arrows in flight. The giveaway is a bird standing with its wings held open to dry.

When: all year, and easiest in the calmer months when they raft up close to shore.

On the rocks and in the weed

04 to 06

Three birds that live on the hard edges rather than the sand. Walk the rocky ends of a beach at low tide and you have a real chance at all three.

Rocky shores, tide poolsBlack body, orange-red bill

Black oystercatcher

Not a true seabird, but the best coastal bird on this list to learn first. It lives on rocky shores and small islands and gives itself away long before you see it, because it is very loud.

Think of it as the black bird with a carrot bill. Black body, long orange-red bill, pink legs. If you remember one rocky shore bird, make it this one.

When: all year, on any rocky headland at low tide.

Rocky shores, docks, islandsWhite wing patch, red feet

Pigeon guillemot

A small black seabird that keeps close to rocky shores, docks and islands rather than heading out to sea. In breeding plumage it is one of the easier small seabirds to name.

Think black bird with red feet. If you see a small dark seabird near rocks and it flashes white on the wing, look at the feet.

When: best in the breeding months of late spring and summer, when the feet and wing patches are brightest.

Rocks, seaweed, tide lineFlips seaweed and small stones

Black turnstone

A dark, stocky shorebird that works the rocks and the weed rather than the open sand. It is easy to walk past, because it is the same colour as everything it stands on.

The behaviour names it: watch for a bird turning over seaweed and flipping small stones to get at what is underneath.

When: strongest on both passages, and present through the winter.

At the tide line, in passage

07 to 08

This is the group the spring trip is for. Go at low tide, when the flats are uncovered and the flocks are down and feeding rather than in the air.

Beaches, mudflats, tide lineSmall, fast, always in flocks

Sandpipers and the small shorebirds

The birds the passage is made of. Western and least sandpipers, sanderlings, dunlin, semipalmated and black-bellied plovers all move through together, and in the peak weeks they come down on the open sand in thousands at a time.

Think tiny runners at the tide line. Sandpipers move in busy flocks, sanderlings chase the waves in and out, and plovers stop and go instead of running without a break.

When: the spring passage, at its heaviest in late April and May, and again from September to November. Low tide.

Good to know. These birds are refuelling on a long flight. A flock put into the air burns exactly what it landed to gain, so keep your distance and keep dogs on a lead.

Mudflats, shoreline, beachesLong down-curved bill

Whimbrel

Much bigger than the sandpipers it feeds alongside, and the one shorebird on this coast a beginner can name from a distance. It works the mudflats and the shoreline in ones and twos rather than in flocks.

The bill does it. Nothing else out there has that long, smooth downward curve.

When: both passages, spring and fall. The curved bill makes it findable even in a busy flock.

On the water in the cold months

09 to 12

The winter arrivals, and the reason a wet November trip is not a wasted one. All four sit on the water rather than the shore, so scan the bays and the sheltered water beyond the surf.

Bays, protected coastDark duck, bold colourful bill

Surf scoter

The common sea duck here through the colder half of the year. Look for groups floating beyond the surf and diving together, often well out from the sand.

A dark duck with a surprisingly bright bill is almost always this one. White patches on the head of the male make it easier still.

When: fall through winter, with the first birds back in September.

Rocky shores, surf zonesPatterned duck in rough water

Harlequin duck

The one duck that chooses the wave-washed rock rather than the calm bay. It is worth walking a rocky headland in winter specifically for this bird.

Males carry white marks and rusty sides that look painted on. Any small duck riding the swell up against the rocks is worth a second look.

When: winter, near rocky, wave-washed shore.

Bays, calm nearshore waterLong body, low in the water

Loons

Common and Pacific loons both turn up in the region on calm water in fall and winter. They sit low, dive smoothly and stay under a long time.

Think quiet spear-billed diver. Long body, pointed bill, no fuss on the surface, and a dive with no splash at the end of it.

When: fall and winter, on the calmer water inside the surf line.

Bays and nearshore waterSlim neck, pointed bill, vanishes

Grebes

Horned, red-necked and western grebes all use the bays and inlets here, and all three are more common outside summer.

Think little floating divers. They go under fast and come up somewhere you were not looking, which is the surest sign you are watching a grebe and not a duck.

When: outside summer, and best on calm water.

Past the surf line, boat only

13 to 18

These six spend their lives away from the sand. From the beach they are dots, and most visitors who see them see them from a wildlife or birding boat around the offshore rocks and islands.

Offshore water, boat toursFlying penguin shape

Common murre

A black and white seabird that spends most of its time out on the ocean. At a distance it can look like a small penguin, but it flies, low and fast, straight over the water.

Look for a black head and back, a white belly, a long pointed bill and a bird that sits low and dives rather than dabbles.

When: summer is the practical season, on a boat or with binoculars from a headland.

Offshore water, boat toursChunky, plain puffin cousin

Rhinoceros auklet

A chunky seabird related to the puffins and far more numerous here. In breeding season the adults carry a small pale horn on the bill, which is where the name comes from.

Think plain puffin cousin: heavier than a murre, less colourful than a puffin, and built entirely for diving.

When: summer, on a wildlife or birding boat.

Offshore rocks and islandsBright bill, pale head tufts

Tufted puffin

The bird most visitors hope for, and the one that most often gets missed, because it is not a bird you see walking a town beach. It sits out around the offshore rocks and islands.

In breeding plumage there is nothing else like it: a large orange bill, a dark body, a pale face and long golden head tufts.

When: summer, and only realistically on a boat tour to the offshore rocks.

Good to know. Puffins are seasonal and offshore. If seeing one is the point of the trip, book a guided birding or wildlife tour rather than hoping from the sand.

Calm nearshore waterSmall seabird, nests in old trees

Marbled murrelet

A small seabird with a connection to the rainforest that nothing else on this list has. It nests high in old-growth trees and flies out to feed at sea, which is why the forest behind the beach and the water in front of it are the same story.

Hard to name with confidence, and worth knowing anyway. Small, chunky, fast in flight, usually on calm water close in.

When: possible all year on calm nearshore water, easiest from a boat.

Offshore waterLong narrow wings, glides

Shearwaters

True offshore birds that glide low over the ocean on long narrow wings, sometimes in large numbers. Sooty, pink-footed and short-tailed shearwaters all pass this coast in season.

Think wind riders. They skim the waves and use the air just above the water like a road, barely beating a wing.

When: in season offshore, most often seen from a boat.

Outer coast, in seasonBig glider with a scoop bill

Brown pelican

The least predictable bird here, and the hardest to miss when it does turn up. Pelicans appear along the outer coast in season, and they are large enough to name from a long way off.

Think big glider with a scoop bill. They look far too heavy to fly and then move down a wave line with no effort at all.

When: seasonal and unpredictable. Watch for low gliding groups over the outer coast.

Before you go

Four things that decide what you see

Almost none of this is about knowing bird names. It is about turning up at the right state of tide, with something to see through, and not putting the flock into the air.

Binoculars change the page

Most seabirds are dots without them, and half the birds on this list are named by a detail you cannot see with the naked eye: a bill horn, a wing patch, the colour of a foot. One pair between two people is enough.

Go at low tide

The flats the shorebirds feed on are only uncovered as the tide drops, and that is when the birds are down and close rather than in the air or gone. Check the day's tide before you pick your hour.

Give a flock its distance

Birds on passage are refuelling for a long flight. Every time a flock is put up it burns what it stopped here to gain, so watch from where you are, keep dogs on a lead, and let the birds decide the distance.

Do not feed the gulls

It makes them bolder, more aggressive around people and less healthy, and it teaches them to work car parks and picnic tables instead of the tide line. This holds for every animal on this coast.

Questions

Asked before people come

Open the ones you need. Everything here is answered in the same words a guide would use on the beach.

When is the best time for birdwatching in Tofino?

Spring and fall are the two migration windows, and late April and May are the strongest weeks of the year. Summer is the season for offshore birds, because the water is calm enough for boats to reach the rocks and islands. Winter is for sea ducks, loons, grebes, gulls, cormorants and eagles. There is no month with nothing in it.

Where is the best place to birdwatch in Tofino?

The Tofino mudflats and Grice Bay for shorebirds, Chesterman Beach, Long Beach and Wickaninnish Beach for the tide line, Tofino Harbour for gulls, cormorants and eagles without leaving town, and Clayoquot Sound by boat for anything offshore.

What seabirds can you see in Tofino?

Gulls, cormorants, common murres, rhinoceros auklets, pigeon guillemots, tufted puffins, shearwaters, sea ducks, loons, grebes, black oystercatchers and bald eagles.

Can you see puffins in Tofino?

Yes. Tufted puffins may be seen near Tofino, especially on summer boat tours near the offshore rocks and islands. They are not usually easy to see from town beaches, so a guided birding or wildlife tour gives you a much better chance.

What are the black birds on the rocks in Tofino?

They may be cormorants, black oystercatchers or pigeon guillemots. Cormorants have long necks and often hold their wings open to dry. Black oystercatchers have bright orange-red bills. Pigeon guillemots are smaller and may show white wing patches.

What are the small birds running on the beach?

Shorebirds, most likely sandpipers, sanderlings, dunlin or plovers. They feed along the tide line and on the mudflats, usually in groups, and they are at their most numerous during the spring and fall passages.

Do you need a boat tour to see seabirds?

Not always. Gulls, cormorants, eagles, shorebirds, sea ducks, loons and grebes are all visible from shore. A boat helps if you want puffins, murres, auklets, shearwaters and the other birds that live past the surf line.

What should I bring for birdwatching in Tofino?

Binoculars, rain gear, warm layers, waterproof shoes, a camera, and a bird app or field guide. The weather changes fast on this coast, and most of the good birding happens standing still in the open.
