Nova Scotia

Halifax

The Halifax Citadel National Historic Site (officially Fort George) stands proudly on a hilltop, overlooking the vibrant city of Halifax and its picturesque harbor. This star-shaped fortress is a living history museum that transports you back to 1869 and played a key role in the defense of the city.

World-Known Landmarks

10 places

1. Halifax Citadel National Historic Site

Noon Gun: A Halifax tradition since 1857. Every day (except Christmas) at exactly 12:00 PM, the 3rd Brigade Royal Artillery fires a 12-pounder cannon. You can watch the gunners prepare and fire from the ramparts.

78th Highlanders re-enactors: Dressed in authentic kilts and feather bonnets, these soldiers perform rifle drills, pipe and drum concerts, and formal Sentry Changes every hour at the front gate.

2. Halifax Public Gardens

The Halifax Public Gardens is one of the oldest Victorian gardens in North America, offering a tranquil escape in the heart of the city. Spanning 16 acres, this enclosed oasis features stunning flower beds, fountains, and historic statues, making it a picturesque spot for both locals and visitors.

The Bandstand: This ornate, cream-and-blue structure is the heart of the gardens. It was built in 1887 to celebrate Queen Victoria’s Golden Jubilee and still hosts free concerts on Sunday afternoons.

The Gates: The main entrance at the corner of Spring Garden Road and South Park Street features massive, wrought-iron gates cast in Scotland. They are adorned with the city’s coat of arms and the crest of the Horticultural Society.

3. Halifax Waterfront Boardwalk

The Halifax Waterfront Boardwalk is the city’s social and cultural heart. Spanning roughly 4 kilometers (about 2.5 miles), it is one of the longest urban boardwalks in the world and serves as a front-row seat to the world’s second-largest natural harbor.

Wave & Interactive Art: The Wave is a massive, blue sculpture that is the boardwalk's most famous photo op. Nearby, you’ll find the Drunken Lamp Posts, three modern art installations that look like they’ve had one too many at the local pub.

Queen’s Marque District: This new, ultra-modern area features stunning architecture, luxury shops, and Rise Again, a series of granite steps that disappear directly into the harbor, perfect for sitting and watching the tide.

4. Maritime Museum of the Atlantic

The Maritime Museum of the Atlantic is Canada’s oldest and largest maritime museum. Located right on the waterfront, it serves as the definitive storyteller of the city’s relationship with the sea, from the golden age of sail to the tragedies that shook the world.

Titanic Exhibit: This is the museum’s most famous draw. Because Halifax was the base for the recovery mission in 1912, the museum holds a hauntingly beautiful collection of artifacts. Don't miss the one of only a few remaining Titanic deck chairs in existence and a pair of tiny leather shoes belonging to the Unknown Child (later identified through DNA).

Halifax Explosion (1917): A massive gallery dedicated to the largest man-made explosion prior to the atomic bomb. It features shrapnel objects, like a window frame with glass still embedded, and tells the story of how the city rebuilt from the ashes.

5. Canadian Museum of Immigration at Pier 21

Often referred to as the Ellis Island of Canada, the Canadian Museum of Immigration at Pier 21 is a powerful National Historic Site. From 1928 to 1971, this very building served as the gateway for nearly one million immigrants beginning their lives in Canada, as well as the departure point for nearly 500,000 Canadian military personnel during World War II.

The Pier 21 Story: Walk through the restored assembly hall and immersive exhibits that recreate the immigrant experience, from the ocean crossing to the medical exams and customs processing.

The Empress of Ireland Exhibit: Learn about Canada’s deadliest maritime disaster through a permanent collection that honors the 1,012 people who lost their lives in 1914.

6. St. Paul's Church

Located at the south end of the Grand Parade in downtown Halifax, St Paul’s Anglican Church is the oldest building in the city and the oldest Protestant place of worship in Canada. Founded in 1749, it stands as a testament to the early British settlement of Halifax and is a significant piece of North American architectural history.

The Silhouette in the Window: One of the church's most famous legends involves a window on the second floor. Following the 1917 Halifax Explosion, a shadow appeared in the glass that resembles the profile of a man; locals often say it is the silhouette of a curate from that era.

Architectural Heritage: Modelled after Marybone Chapel in London, it features a classic Georgian design. You can still see a piece of a wooden window frame from the Halifax Explosion embedded in the interior walls of the vestibule.

7. Alexander Keith's Brewery

Alexander Keith’s Brewery is a Halifax landmark where visitors can experience one of Canada’s oldest breweries in a beautifully preserved 1820 building. Founded by the three-time mayor of Halifax, Alexander Keith, whose brewing legacy has become an integral part of Nova Scotia’s history. Located in a historic ironstone building on Lower Water Street, it offers a theatrical glimpse into the city's brewing heritage.

Theatrical Brewery Tour: This is not your average technical tour. Actors in full Victorian period costume lead you through the brewery, sharing stories of 1863 Halifax, singing traditional songs, and teaching you about the brewing process of the time.

Stag's Head Tavern: The tour concludes in this authentic underground tavern located within the brewery's cavernous basement. Here, you can sample various Keith's brews while enjoying live maritime music and traditional games.

8. St. Mary's Basilica

Saint Mary's Cathedral Basilica is a striking cornerstone of Halifax’s religious and architectural history, located at the busy intersection of Spring Garden Road and Barrington Street. Built in the Gothic Revival style, it is the cathedral church of the Roman Catholic Archdiocese of Halifax-Yarmouth and is designated as a National Historic Site of Canada.

The Granite Spire: The cathedral boasts the tallest granite spire in North America, a magnificent architectural feat that dominates the local skyline and serves as a landmark for those navigating the downtown core.

Stained Glass Masterpieces: The interior is illuminated by breathtaking stained glass windows that depict various biblical scenes and saints, creating a tranquil and colorful atmosphere even on foggy Atlantic days.

9. Old Town Clock

The Old Town Clock is one of Halifax's most enduring and recognizable symbols. Perched on the eastern slope of Citadel Hill, it has kept time for the city since 1803. The clock was a gift from Prince Edward, Duke of Kent, who served as the commander-in-chief of British forces in North America and was famously obsessed with punctuality.

Palladian Architecture: The structure features a classic white circular tower set atop a copper-roofed rectangular building. Its design is a prime example of the Palladian style that was popular in the British Empire during the early 19th century.

The Original Mechanism: Remarkably, the clock still uses much of its original wind-up mechanism, which was crafted by the House of Vuillamy, the royal clockmakers in London. It is still meticulously maintained by Parks Canada staff today.

10. Government House

Government House is the official residence of the Lieutenant Governor of Nova Scotia and stands as one of the oldest viceregal residences in North America. Completed in 1805, this elegant stone mansion has hosted members of the Royal Family for over two centuries and remains a premier example of Adam-style Georgian architecture in Canada.

State Rooms and Art: The interior is filled with period antiques, portraits of British monarchs, and a significant collection of silver. Visitors often admire the grand staircase and the drawing room where formal receptions are held.

Royal Connection: As a functioning royal residence, it features a dedicated suite for the reigning monarch. You can see the Royal Standard flying from the roof whenever the Lieutenant Governor or a member of the Royal Family is in residence.

Art and History Lovers

10 places

1. Art Gallery of Nova Scotia

The Art Gallery of Nova Scotia is the largest art museum in Atlantic Canada, housing a premier collection of over 18,000 works. It serves as a vital cultural hub, celebrating both the traditional heritage and contemporary spirit of the region.

Maud Lewis House: The gallery’s most beloved exhibit is the actual tiny, brightly painted house of folk artist Maud Lewis. Visitors can step inside the fully restored structure, which she covered in cheerful birds, flowers, and animals despite her physical struggles.

Permanent Collection: Explore a vast array of Atlantic Canadian art, ranging from historical portraiture and landscapes to Inuit stone carvings and modern sculptures.

2. HMCS Sackville (Historic Warship)

The HMCS Sackville is a crown jewel of Canada’s naval history, serving as the country's oldest warship and the last surviving Flower-class corvette from the Second World War. Moored prominently along the waterfront, it serves as a moving memorial to the Royal Canadian Navy and the Battle of the Atlantic.

Battle of the Atlantic Experience: Visitors can step back into 1941 to see how sailors lived and worked during the longest continuous military campaign of WWII. The ship is meticulously restored to its wartime configuration, from the cramped mess decks to the bridge.

The Convoy Story: The museum highlights the critical role these small ships played in escorting merchant convoys across the treacherous Atlantic, providing a visceral look at the North Atlantic Run.

3. Fairview Lawn Cemetery (Titanic Gravesite)

The Fairview Lawn Cemetery is a site of international pilgrimage, known primarily as the final resting place for 121 victims of the RMS Titanic disaster. While a somber location, it is a beautifully maintained and peaceful landscape that offers a profound connection to one of the 20th century's greatest maritime tragedies.

Titanic Graves: The most visited section features three long lines of granite headstones. Most of these markers were paid for by the White Star Line and are dated April 15, 1912.

Unknown Child Monument: For decades, this grave stood as a symbol of all the lost children of the Titanic. Although DNA testing in 2007 identified the child as Sidney Leslie Goodwin, the monument remains a deeply emotional focal point for visitors.

4. C.S.S. Acadia

The C.S.S. Acadia is a Grand Old Lady of the sea and one of Canada’s most significant historic vessels. Permanently moored at the Maritime Museum of the Atlantic, this retired hydrographic research ship has a resume unlike any other: she is the only vessel still afloat that survived the 1917 Halifax Explosion and served in the Royal Canadian Navy during both World Wars.

Step Back to 1913: Walk the teak decks of a ship that looks much as she did when she was launched over 110 years ago. You can explore the beautifully preserved Captain’s Quarters, complete with period furniture and brass fittings that reflect the Edwardian era.

The Engine Room: Descend into the heart of the ship to see the massive, immaculately maintained triple-expansion steam engine. It is a masterclass in industrial-age engineering and remains a favorite for gearheads and history buffs alike.

5. Old Burying Ground

The Old Burying Ground is a significant historic site in downtown Halifax that served as the city's primary cemetery from its founding in 1749 until 1844. This designated National Historic Site of Canada offers a quiet, reflective space that chronicles the early history and genealogy of the city's settlers.

Historical Significance: It is the final resting place of approximately 12,000 people, including many of Halifax's earliest residents, British soldiers, and sailors.

Artistic Gravestones: The site is renowned for its collection of 18th and early 19th-century headstones, which feature intricate carvings of cherubs, death's heads, and other traditional motifs that reflect the era's views on mortality.

6. McNabs Island Provincial Park

McNabs and Lawlor Islands Provincial Park is a hidden gem located at the entrance of the Halifax Harbour. As the largest island in the harbour, it offers a rugged escape into nature combined with fascinating military and social history, making it a favorite for hikers and history buffs alike.

Fort Ives and Military Ruins: Explore the remains of several coastal defense batteries, including Fort Ives, which served as a crucial part of the city's defense system from the mid-19th century through World War II.

Hiking and Beachcombing: The island features over 22 kilometers of hiking trails that wind through forests and along the shoreline. Visitors often head to Maugers Beach for spectacular views of the harbour and the lighthouse.

7. Province House

Province House is the oldest legislative building in Canada and a stunning example of Palladian architecture. Completed in 1819, it was famously described by Charles Dickens as looking like a short-lived palace of ivory, and it continues to serve as the seat of the Nova Scotia House of Assembly.

Legislative Assembly Chamber: Step into the room where Canadian democracy was shaped. This ornate chamber is where responsible government was first established in the British Empire outside of the UK in 1848.

Red Chamber: Formerly the home of the Legislative Council (the upper house), this room is renowned for its portraits of King George III and Queen Charlotte, as well as its intricate plasterwork and historical artifacts.

8. Georges Island National Historic Site

Georges Island National Historic Site is a teardrop-shaped island in the heart of Halifax Harbour that only recently opened to the general public. Historically used as a defensive fortification, a prison, and a tragic internment camp for Acadians, it now offers some of the most unique underground perspectives of the city's military past.

Fort Charlotte & Tunnel Tours: The main attraction is the network of dark, stone tunnels beneath Fort Charlotte. Guided tours (included with admission) lead you through the magazine complex where gunpowder was once stored in bomb-proof safety.

The Acadian & Mi'kmaq History: Visit the interpretive panels that share the island's difficult history as an internment camp for Acadians during the Great Upheaval (1755, 1764) and its significance to the Mi'kmaq, who call it Elpaqkwitk.

9. York Redoubt National Historic Site

Perched on a high bluff overlooking the entrance to the Halifax Harbour, the York Redoubt National Historic Site is a formidable 200-year-old fortification that served as a key element of the city's defense system until the end of World War II. It offers a more rugged and sprawling experience than the Citadel, with a focus on coastal defense and panoramic Atlantic vistas.

Coastal Defense Batteries: Explore the remnants of massive rifled muzzle-loading guns and concrete searchlight emplacements that were designed to protect the harbour from enemy ships.

Command Post View: The site offers some of the most spectacular, unobstructed views of the Halifax Harbour, McNabs Island, and the open ocean, making it a premier spot for ship-watching.

10. Halifax Explosion Memorial Bell Tower

The Halifax Explosion Memorial Bell Tower stands as a solemn reminder of the tragic 1917 Halifax Explosion that devastated the city. Located at Fort Needham Memorial Park, near the former Richmond District and close to the blast’s epicentre, the tower commemorates the lives lost and the resilience Halifax showed in the aftermath.

Its significance comes from its connection to one of the deadliest explosions in history, which killed nearly 2,000 people and injured thousands more. Today, visitors come here to reflect, pay tribute to those affected, and better understand this defining chapter in Halifax’s history.

The Memorial Bells: The tower houses a carillon of ten bells, which are rung annually during the memorial ceremony at 9:04 AM on December 6th, marking the exact moment of the explosion.

Outdoor Attractions

8 places

1. Peggy’s Cove Lighthouse

The Peggy’s Point Lighthouse (widely known as Peggy’s Cove Lighthouse) stands as a stark, white sentinel against the rugged Atlantic coastline. Perched atop massive, wave-worn granite rocks, this iconic red-and-white tower has become the most famous symbol of Nova Scotia and one of the most photographed landmarks in all of Canada.

The Lighthouse and Granite Barrens: Walk across the vast, undulating granite rocks that surround the tower. These unique geological formations were carved by retreating glaciers thousands of years ago and offer a dramatic, lunar-like landscape for exploration.

Fishing Village: Wander through the actual village of Peggy’s Cove. It is a tiny, active community where you can see colorful fish stores, weathered wharves, and lobster traps stacked high against the backdrop of the crashing surf.

2. Halifax Harbour Ferry

The Halifax Harbour Ferry is the oldest continuously operating saltwater ferry service in North America, having begun in 1752. For the price of a bus fare, it provides a scenic 15-minute mini-cruise across one of the world’s deepest natural harbors, connecting the bustling Halifax Waterfront with the trendy Alderney Landing in downtown Dartmouth.

Skyline Views: Enjoy the best panoramic view of the Halifax skyline, including the Citadel, the Old Town Clock, and the rising glass towers of the Queen’s Marque, all from the open-top deck.

The Fleet of Heroes: Look for the names on the vessels. Each ferry is named after a local legend, such as Vincent Coleman (the heroic dispatcher who warned trains during the 1917 Halifax Explosion) or Viola Desmond (the civil rights icon featured on the Canadian $10 bill).

3. Sir Sandford Fleming Park (The Dingle)

Sir Sandford Fleming Park, affectionately known by locals as The Dingle, is a 95-acre urban oasis donated to the city in 1908 by the man who gave the world Standard Time. Nestled along the Northwest Arm, this park combines lush woodlands, saltwater beaches, and a towering monument that commemorates Nova Scotia’s role in democratic history.

The Dingle Tower: The park’s centerpiece is a massive Italianate-style stone tower built between 1908 and 1912. It was erected to mark the 150th anniversary of the establishment of representative government in Nova Scotia, the first of its kind in the British Commonwealth.

The Bronze Lions: Guarding the entrance to the tower are two magnificent bronze lions, donated by the Royal Colonial Institute of London. They are replicas of the famous lions in Trafalgar Square and are a favorite spot for children to climb and take photos.

4. Long Lake Provincial Park

Long Lake Provincial Park is a massive 2,000-hectare wilderness escape located just minutes from the urban core of Halifax. It offers a perfect balance between rugged Nova Scotian backcountry and accessible, well-groomed trails. Whether you are looking for a high-intensity trail run or a quiet afternoon by the water, this park is the city’s favorite backyard for outdoor enthusiasts.

The Lake Loop: Explore the 5-kilometer groomed crusher-dust trail that circles a portion of the park. It is wide, flat, and perfect for strollers, bicycles, and joggers who want a scenic view of the water without the technical terrain of a mountain hike.

Swimming and Sunbathing: During the summer months, the lake’s clean, clear water makes it one of the most popular swimming holes in the region. There are several rocky outcrops and a small sandy area near the main entrance that serve as makeshift beaches.

5. Hemlock Ravine Park

Hemlock Ravine Park is a hidden natural gem located in the Bedford Basin area of Halifax. Once part of the sprawling country estate of Prince Edward, Duke of Kent (the father of Queen Victoria), this 200-acre park is famous for its towering old-growth trees and a heart-shaped pond that serves as a romantic tribute from the city’s past.

The Heart-Shaped Pond: This is the park's most famous feature. Originally built by Prince Edward in the 1790s as a gift for his mistress, Julie St. Laurent, the pond remains a perfectly shaped heart. It is a quiet, reflective spot surrounded by benches and a favorite for wedding photography.

Old-Growth Hemlocks: Hike through the Ravine to see massive Eastern Hemlock trees, some of which are over 300 years old. These giants escaped the massive 1917 Halifax Explosion and provide a thick, cooling canopy even on the hottest summer days.

6. Emera Oval

The Emera Oval is a massive outdoor refrigerated ice surface located on the Halifax Common. Originally built as a temporary venue for the 2011 Canada Winter Games, it was so beloved by locals that it was made a permanent fixture. Equivalent in size to three NHL hockey rinks, it is the largest outdoor artificially frozen ice surface east of Quebec City, offering free year-round recreation in the heart of the city.

Ice Skating (Winter): From December to March, the Oval is a winter wonderland. Thousands of skaters of all skill levels glide around the 400-meter track. There are designated lanes for fast speed skaters and inner areas for those just learning.

Rollerblading and Biking (Summer): Once the ice melts, the concrete surface is perfect for inline skating, roller skating, and cycling. It is a smooth, safe environment away from car traffic, making it ideal for kids and beginners.

7. Point Pleasant Park

Point Pleasant Park is a sprawling 75-hectare wooded oasis situated at the southern tip of the Halifax peninsula. It is a unique blend of natural forest, coastal scenery, and deep military history. Once a key component of the city’s defense system, the park is now a beloved retreat for runners, dog walkers, and anyone looking to breathe in the fresh salt air of the Atlantic.

The Prince of Wales Tower: This National Historic Site is the oldest Martello tower in North America (built in 1796). Its thick stone walls were designed to defend British batteries from a sea-based French attack. While you can't always go inside, its exterior is a focal point for history buffs.

Fortification Ruins: As you explore the 39 kilometers of trails, you’ll stumble upon the haunting stone remains of several coastal forts, including Fort Ogilvie and the Cambridge Battery. Many of these ruins are open for exploration and offer elevated views of the harbor entrance.

8. Angus L. Macdonald Bridge

The Angus L. Macdonald Bridge stands as a majestic suspension span, arching over the deep blue waters of the Halifax Harbour. Connecting the downtown cores of Halifax and Dartmouth, this iconic Old Bridge is a vital transportation link and a premier vantage point for capturing the maritime essence of Nova Scotia.

The Pedestrian Walkway: Located on the south side of the bridge, this high-altitude path offers the most famous panoramic view in the city. From 154 feet above the water, you can see the Halifax waterfront, George’s Island, and the sprawling Atlantic Ocean beyond.

Navy Ship Spotting: The bridge passes directly over HMCS Dockyard, the headquarters of the Royal Canadian Navy’s Atlantic Fleet. It is one of the few places in the world where you can walk directly over active frigates, coastal defense vessels, and visiting NATO warships.

Other Interesting Places

10 places

1. Halifax Central Library

The Halifax Central Library is a striking architectural landmark that serves as the community living room for the city. Opened in 2014 and designed as a stack of four glass boxes, it has won numerous international awards, including the Governor General’s Medal in Architecture. More than just a place for books, it is a vibrant cultural hub in the heart of the Spring Garden Road shopping district.

The Fifth Floor Living Room: Visit the top floor, which is a massive glass cantilever that hangs dramatically over the street. This quiet lounge offers floor-to-ceiling windows with the best free indoor views of Citadel Hill and the downtown core.

The Escher-esque Atrium: Walk through the center of the building to see the sculptural, criss-crossing staircases and walkways. The five-story skylit atrium is a masterpiece of light and geometry.

2. Historic Properties

The Historic Properties (also known as Privateers' Wharf) is a collection of stone and timber warehouses that date back to the late 1700s and early 1800s. Originally the base of operations for swashbuckling privateers and merchants during the Napoleonic Wars and the War of 1812, these three city blocks were saved from demolition in the 1960s. Today, they form a charming National Historic Site filled with boutiques, pubs, and restaurants along the Halifax waterfront.

Walk the Cobblestone Streets: Wander through the narrow alleys between buildings like the Privateer Warehouse (c. 1790) and the Red Store (1812). The architecture features original ironstone walls and heavy timber beams that make you feel like you’ve stepped into the Age of Sail.

Spot the Privateer History: Look for the old stocks and pillory, reproductions of the 18th-century punishment devices, located near the wharf. It’s a popular (if slightly grim) spot for a classic Halifax photo.

3. Royal Nova Scotia International Tattoo

The Royal Nova Scotia International Tattoo is a world-class spectacular that blends military tradition with high-energy entertainment. Held annually at the Scotiabank Centre, it is the largest indoor show of its kind in the world, featuring over 2,000 performers from across the globe. Part Broadway show, part military display, it is a fast-paced journey through history, music, and jaw-dropping acrobatics.

The Massed Pipes and Drums: Experience the spine-tingling moment when hundreds of bagpipers and drummers march onto the arena floor in a powerful display of Celtic heritage.

Military Skill Displays: Watch elite units perform high-speed drills and dangerous stunts. A fan favorite is the Naval Gun Run, where teams race to dismantle and reassemble massive field guns over obstacles.

4. Halifax Seaport Farmer's Market

The Halifax Seaport Farmers' Market is the oldest continuously operating farmers' market in North America, having been founded in 1750. Now located in the historic Seaport District at Pavilion 23, this bustling waterfront market is a vibrant celebration of Nova Scotian agriculture, craftsmanship, and global flavors, all housed under one roof next to the Atlantic Ocean.

The Global Food Court: Arrive hungry to sample an incredible array of international cuisines. From Masita’s Korean BBQ Hot Dogs to Pinoy’s Best steamed buns and fresh Acadian seafood chowder, the market is a culinary map of Halifax’s diverse community.

Artisan Alley: Browse the stalls of local makers for unique Atlantic Canadian souvenirs. You’ll find everything from hand-poured sea salt sourced from St. Margaret’s Bay to rugged woolens, maritime-inspired jewelry, and original folk art.

5. Halifax City Hall

Halifax City Hall stands as a magnificent example of late Victorian architecture and remains the heart of municipal government for the Halifax Regional Municipality. Completed in 1890 on the site of the old Dalhousie College, this National Historic Site is famous for its striking cream-and-red sandstone exterior and its iconic clock tower that defines the northern end of the Grand Parade.

The Six O’Clock Clock: Look up at the north face of the silver-domed clock tower. You will notice the clock is permanently fixed at 9:04. This is a solemn memorial to the exact moment of the 1917 Halifax Explosion, a tribute that has remained unchanged for over a century.

Grand Parade Square: Explore the historic military parade ground directly in front of City Hall. It serves as a civic living room, hosting everything from the cenotaph Remembrance Day ceremonies to the Grand Parade Concert Series during the summer months.

6. Garrison Brewing Co.

Garrison Brewing Co. is a cornerstone of the East Coast craft beer scene and a pioneer in the Halifax Seaport District. Since 1997, they have been brewing fiercely independent beers in historic buildings near the harbor. Their main Seaport taproom, housed in a former 1929 immigration annex, is a vibrant, industrial-chic space where the aroma of hops meets the salty Atlantic air.

The Seaport Taproom Experience: Grab a seat in the high-ceilinged taproom and watch the active brewhouse in action. You can sample a flight of five distinct beers while looking out at the cruise ship terminals just steps away.

Sample the Flagships: Try the Tall Ship East Coast Ale, a crisp local classic, or the Irish Red Ale. For those who prefer bold flavors, the award-winning Juicy! New England IPA is a fan favorite for its tropical, hazy notes.

7. Neptune Theatre

Neptune Theatre stands as Atlantic Canada’s largest professional regional theatre, a cultural cornerstone located in the heart of downtown Halifax. Since opening its doors in 1963 with a mission to bring world-class performance to the East Coast, it has become a vibrant hub for Broadway-style musicals, provocative dramas, and local premieres, all housed within its sleek, modern facility on Argyle Street.

The Fountain Hall Experience: Attend a production on the main stage, known as Fountain Hall. With 521 seats, it offers the big theatre feel with incredible acoustics. In Spring 2026, don't miss the blockbuster production of Come From Away (running April 1, June 7), the hit musical about the generosity of Newfoundlanders during 9/11.

The Scotiabank Stage: For a more intimate experience, head to the 200-seat studio stage. This space is dedicated to contemporary works and edgy, experimental plays. In 2026, look for solo performances like Vanya starring Canadian theatre legend Daniel MacIvor.

8. Dalhousie University

Dalhousie University is one of Canada's oldest and most prestigious universities, founded in 1818 by the 9th Earl of Dalhousie. The Studley Campus, in particular, is a landmark of academic history, featuring grand stone buildings and mature tree-lined pathways that create a classic Ivy League atmosphere in the heart of Halifax.

The Henry Hicks Building: This is the iconic centerpiece of the campus. Completed in 1915, its striking limestone facade and clock tower are a prime example of the university's enduring architectural legacy.

The Quad: The central green space is the heart of student life. It is surrounded by historic structures and provides a peaceful, park-like setting that is open for the public to stroll through.

9. Halifax Brewery Farmers Market

The Halifax Brewery Farmers' Market is a charming, community-run cooperative tucked inside the 200-year-old Alexander Keith’s Brewery building. While the larger Seaport Market is known for its modern, airy feel, the Brewery Market is celebrated for its old-world atmosphere, featuring granite walls, ironstone courtyards, and hidden rooms that create one of the most intimate shopping experiences in Canada.

The Historic Courtyard: Wander through the sunlit atrium and winding corridors of a building constructed in 1837. The mix of heritage stone and glass makes it feel more like a hidden European village than a typical market.

Breakfast in the Brewery: This market is a local favorite for a slow Saturday morning. Grab a fresh coffee from the on-site café and a hot, made-from-scratch meal, options range from authentic Indian curries and Ethiopian platters to classic egg sandwiches and gluten-free pastries.

10. Casino Nova Scotia

Casino Nova Scotia is Atlantic Canada’s premier gaming and entertainment destination, famously built on a caisson-based structure that extends over the harbor waters like a modern-day wharf. Located at the northern end of the Halifax Waterfront Boardwalk, it offers a Vegas-style experience with a distinct maritime flair, serving as a hub for nightlife, live music, and waterfront dining.

The Gaming Floor: Explore over 40,000 square feet of excitement featuring more than 600 slot machines and over 30 live table games, including Blackjack, Roulette, and Baccarat.

The Bruce Guthro Theatre: Catch a show in this premiere 650-seat venue (formerly the Schooner Showroom). In March 2026, the theater is hosting a legendary lineup including The Irish Descendants (March 14), Bruce Guthro’s Songwriters’ Circle (March 28), and the musical spectacular Music of the Night (March 31).
