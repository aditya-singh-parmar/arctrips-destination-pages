Whale watching, Tofino

Wildlife tours in Tofino

Whales, bears and seabirds. Which tours are worth booking, what you actually see from the boat, and what the shore gives you for free.

Marit Sandoval Four trips out of Tofino Harbour, March to September Updated 21 July 2026
In this guide What you see When to go Zodiac or covered From the shore Blows and flukes Booking and kit Questions

What you are actually looking for

Tofino is one of the best places on Vancouver Island to see wild animals. It sits beside Clayoquot Sound, a rich coastal area with ocean, islands, beaches, rainforest and rocky shorelines. Almost everything on a tour boat here comes down to that geography: deep water close in, shallow feeding ground behind the islands, and a harbour you can leave from in almost any weather.

Three whales carry most trips. Gray whales come past on migration in spring, humpbacks work the feeding water through summer and into September, and orcas turn up often enough to be worth hoping for and not often enough to be worth planning around. Alongside them you get sea otters, sea lions hauled out on the rocks, harbour seals, river otters, bald eagles, great blue herons and more seabirds than most people expect.

Bears are a separate trip. Operators run them into the calm inlet water behind town, around Grice Bay, and they are timed to a low tide rather than to a time of day, because that is when black bears come down to turn rocks over on the flats. If you want both, book them on different days. They are not the same boat and they are not the same water.

Good to know

Nothing on the water is guaranteed. Operators pick routes on the day from weather, swell and recent sightings, and the honest ones will tell you when the sea state means a shorter loop closer in.

When to go

Tours run eight months of the twelve, one of the longest seasons on this coast. That is why March in Tofino books out while the rain is still falling.

When should you go?

Tours run March to October. Go in April or May for gray whales, August or September for humpbacks. November to February the boats stop and the storms take over.

Zodiac or covered boat

This is the only real decision you have to make, and it changes the day more than the operator does.

  • A covered boat is better for comfort, families and cooler days. There is a heated cabin to retreat into, a washroom, and somewhere to put a camera down. You sit higher, which helps you spot a blow at distance.
  • A zodiac is better for travellers who want a faster, more open-air adventure. You are lower, closer to the water and colder, and the boat can turn and hold in places a bigger hull will not. Nobody on a zodiac stays dry in a swell.
  • A smaller tour or private charter is the photographers' answer, and the answer for a group that wants the skipper's attention rather than a commentary.

Many tours are about two and a half to three hours, but timing can vary, and the exact length is the operator's to set. There is no perfect time of day either. Morning trips may have calmer water, but wildlife can be active at different times, and a good skipper will chase the report rather than the clock.

If you are travelling with someone who gets seasick, take the covered boat and take the tablets before boarding rather than after. The swell out past the islands is longer and slower than it looks from the dock.

Watching from the shore

Yes, sometimes whales can be seen from shore. But a guided boat tour gives you a much better chance of seeing whales and learning about them, and it is worth being straight about that before you spend a morning on a headland with binoculars.

If you want to try anyway, these are the places on this coast where people actually stand and scan. Six are Tofino beaches, where you get the horizon. Six are down the road on the Ucluelet side, where the rock gets you height over the water. What you are looking for at that range is not a whale. It is a blow, hanging for a second or two against the grey.

Check the tide first

At Chesterman Beach, always check the tide before walking out toward Frank Island. The water can come in and cover the sandspit while you are out there.

Grice Bay, on the inlet side, is the opposite kind of water: calm, shallow and sheltered. Choose it for still scenery and for the birds, not for whales and not for storms.

Blows, backs and flukes

Most people spend the first half hour of a tour looking for the wrong thing. Whales do not pose. What you get is a sequence, and once you can read it you stop missing things.

A blow means the whale has surfaced to breathe. It is often the first sign people see during whale watching, and on a still morning you can hear it before anyone points. Whales show their backs when they surface to breathe, travel or prepare for another dive. That long dark roll is the most common thing anyone sees all day.

If a whale lifts its tail fluke before disappearing, it usually means the whale is making a deeper dive.

A fluke is not a goodbye. It usually means a deeper dive, and the whale may surface again nearby after several minutes, or come up a long way off.

Marit Sandoval, on the water off Clayoquot Sound

So when the boat cuts its engine and everyone goes quiet, that is the skipper counting. Gray whales feeding in the shallows will often stay in one patch of water for an hour. Humpbacks travelling will not. The difference between the two is the difference between a five minute stop and the rest of your trip.

Gray whales pass here as they travel north from winter breeding and calving areas in Baja California, Mexico, toward northern feeding grounds near Alaska and the Arctic. The main spring migration period is usually March to May. Early sightings may happen in February, and some whales may still be present into early summer, which is the overlap that makes May the strongest single month on this coast.

Booking, and what to take

Tours here fill first at the two ends of the season: the second half of March, when the gray whales are coming back through and the Pacific Rim Whale Festival is on, and the middle of August. Between those, a day or two of notice is usually enough. Sailings do get cancelled for swell, so leave a spare morning in the trip if you can only do this once.

  • Layers, and a windproof shell. It is consistently colder on the water than on the sand, in every month of the year.
  • Binoculars. The single biggest upgrade to the day, on the boat and off it.
  • Motion sickness tablets, taken before boarding. Not after.
  • A camera you can hold with one hand. The other hand is for the rail.
  • Sunglasses, even in the rain. Glare off grey water is what tires your eyes out by hour two.
  • A national park pass if the same day takes you down to Long Beach, Wickaninnish or Florencia Bay.
